Serious Assault Charges Queensland — Section 340 Criminal Code

Serious assault under section 340 of the Queensland Criminal Code is what a common assault becomes when the alleged victim is a police officer, public officer, person over 60, or person with a disability — or where the offender acts with intent to resist arrest. The maximum penalty ranges from 7 to 14 years imprisonment depending on the variant.

What is serious assault?

Defined by who, not how hard.

Serious assault is one of the most misunderstood charges in the Queensland Criminal Code. The conduct itself is often minor. The classification is what bites.

StatuteCriminal Code 1899 (Qld) s 340
Year introduced1899 · expanded 2014, 2020
TypeIndictable · either-way
Max penalty7 or 14 years

Section 340 of the Criminal Code 1899 (Qld) doesn't punish a different kind of violence. It punishes the same kind of violence — pushing, striking, threatening, spitting — when the person on the receiving end falls within a protected group, or when it happens in specified circumstances. The offence exists to shield the people society relies on to do difficult, dangerous or vulnerable work, and to protect older Queenslanders and people with disabilities from being targeted.

The most common version of the charge is assault on a police officer in the execution of their duty. Spitting at an officer during an arrest, kicking out while being put in handcuffs, pushing past an officer to leave the scene, biting during a search — every one of those sits inside section 340. So does pushing a paramedic responding to a triple-zero call, shoving a transit officer at a station, or grabbing the wrist of a corrections officer in a watchhouse. The conduct does not have to cause injury. Even minor contact qualifies.

"What separates section 340 from a common assault isn't the force. It's the uniform — or the age — of the person on the other end."

The other variants follow the same logic. An assault on a person aged 60 or over is automatically serious assault — there's no requirement that the offender knew the person's age, and there's no minimum force threshold. An assault on a person who relies on a guide dog, wheelchair or other remedial device is serious assault. So is an assault while in company with one or more other people, and an assault committed with intent to resist or prevent the lawful arrest of yourself or someone else.

Because the charge turns on circumstance rather than severity, people are routinely charged with serious assault for incidents they themselves describe as a scuffle. A push during an argument with an older neighbour. A reflex movement while being arrested for something else. A spit in frustration in the back of a divisional van. The paperwork that lands in your hands says "serious assault — up to 14 years," and it is genuinely terrifying. But the maximum is rarely the outcome — and how the matter is presented, negotiated and ultimately resolved is exactly the kind of work we do every week.

Elements of the offence

What the prosecution must prove.

Every serious assault charge requires the prosecution to establish each element beyond reasonable doubt. Where any one element falls over, the charge fails — or drops back to common assault. This is the framework we use to read every brief.

01

An assault occurred

The defendant struck, applied force to, or threatened force against the complainant — without their consent. "Force" includes minimal contact: a push, a tap, a poke, a spit. There need be no injury.

02

Protected category or specified circumstance

The complainant must fall within a protected category — police, public officer, person 60+, person with a disability — or the assault must have occurred in a specified circumstance such as being committed in company or with intent to resist arrest.

03

Officer acting in execution of duty

For police and public officer variants, the prosecution must prove the officer was actively performing their duties at the time. An off-duty officer, or one acting outside their lawful authority, does not satisfy this element.

04

Lawfulness of arrest or detention

For "resisting arrest" variants, the underlying arrest or detention must have been lawful. If it wasn't — for example, no reasonable suspicion, failure to identify as police — the s 340 charge collapses to common assault at most.

05

No consent, no lawful excuse

The Crown must negate consent, self-defence, accident and provocation where any of those defences are squarely raised on the evidence. The standard is beyond reasonable doubt — the highest in the legal system.

Note

Knowledge is not an element

It is not a defence that the defendant didn't realise the person was a police officer or didn't know they were over 60. The prosecution does not have to prove the defendant knew. This is one of the reasons s 340 catches people by surprise.

What makes an assault 'serious'

Seven circumstances that change the charge.

Section 340 lists the circumstances that elevate a common assault to serious assault. Some are about who the alleged victim is. Some are about when or how the assault occurred. Each one pushes the maximum penalty up — and changes which court hears the matter.

01

Police officer on duty

Assault of a police officer in the execution of their duty — including biting, spitting, kicking or pushing during arrest. The most-prosecuted variant of s 340. Prosecution leads body-worn camera, the QP9 and the officer's statement.

Example: kicking out at an officer while being placed in handcuffs after a public-order incident.
02

Public officer

Paramedics, ED nurses, corrections officers, transit officers, parking inspectors, council workers and other public officials acting in the course of their duty. Prosecution typically argues the role of the worker is intuitive and that the defendant should have known.

Example: pushing a paramedic responding to a call-out, even where no injury results.
03

Person aged 60 or over

Any assault where the complainant is 60 years or older becomes serious assault, regardless of context. The prosecution does not need to prove you knew their age — only that they were 60.

Example: a shove during an argument with an older neighbour over a parking dispute.
04

Person with a disability

Assault of a person who relies on a guide dog, wheelchair or other remedial device — recognised under s 340. Argued whenever a mobility aid or assistance animal was visibly in use during the incident.

Example: minor contact during an argument with a person using a mobility aid.
05

While in company

Where the assault was committed in company with one or more other people — group dynamics elevate the conduct. Often raised in pub, party and street-fight matters where multiple people are charged jointly.

Example: two friends jointly involved in pushing a third person during a confrontation.
06

Resisting / preventing arrest

Assault committed with intent to resist or prevent the lawful arrest or detention of yourself or another person. Prosecution typically pairs this with obstruct police (s 790 PPRA) on the same set of facts.

Example: pulling away forcefully during an arrest for a separate offence.
07

Corrections / unlawful conspiracy

Prisoners who assault working corrective services officers are separately covered under s 340(2). Section 340(1)(f) also captures assault committed in pursuance of an unlawful conspiracy — a less common variant included for completeness with the legislation.

Example: an assault on a corrections officer during a watchhouse search or transfer.

In Practice

Probation, fines, community service.

First-time serious assault police matters dealt with in the Magistrates Court commonly result in probation orders, community service, or fines. Imprisonment is more likely where bodily fluid, weapons, or injury are involved, or where the offender has relevant prior history.

Suspended Sentences

Wholly suspended is on the table.

A wholly suspended sentence of imprisonment is available and commonly imposed for mid-range serious assault matters — particularly where there's strong rehabilitation, character material and an early plea.

Section 12 PSA

No-conviction outcomes are achievable.

Under section 12 of the Penalties and Sentences Act 1992 (Qld), no-conviction orders are available for lower-end s 340 matters — particularly first offences with no significant injury. We invest in the application early.

Which court will hear my matter?

Magistrates Court or District Court?

This is the question every person charged with serious assault asks within 24 hours. The honest answer is: the prosecution decides, not you — but how the matter is presented early can change which way they go.

Summary jurisdiction

Magistrates Court

Many s 340 matters CAN be dealt with summarily in the Magistrates Court at the prosecution's election under s 552A of the Criminal Code. This is almost always the better outcome — faster, cheaper, lower sentencing range.

  • Heard by a Magistrate alone — no jury
  • Lower-range maximum penalty applies (often capped at 3 years for summary)
  • Resolution typically in weeks to a few months
  • Pleas, trials and sentences all run in this court
  • Most assault police matters without bodily fluid stay here
Typical timeline6–16 weeks
Higher jurisdiction

District Court

Where the conduct is more serious, the prosecution elects to commit the matter to the District Court for trial or sentence. Committal proceedings precede this — a Magistrates Court process where the brief is tested before the matter is sent up.

  • Heard by a Judge — jury for trials
  • Full 14-year maximum in play for indictable variants
  • Adds 8–12 months to the timeline after committal
  • Required for bodily fluid, weapons, in-company, BH causing
  • Sentencing range is materially higher
Typical timeline9–18 months

Defences we run on serious assault

Real defences. Tested every week.

We review the brief of evidence, body-worn camera footage, CCTV and witness statements before advising on your options. The right defence on a serious assault matter often turns on what the police footage actually shows — not what the QP9 says.

s 271 / 272

Self-defence

Available even against police where the use of force by the officer was unlawful or disproportionate. The prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt. Body-worn footage and witness statements often reframe what looked like assault into reasonable response.

s 269

Provocation

Partial defence reducing intent. Considered carefully where there has been sustained antagonism prior to the alleged assault, or where racial, sexual or homophobic abuse preceded the contact.

s 23

Accident / lack of intent

For example, contact made while struggling against an unlawful restraint, where intent to assault is denied. Body-worn camera review is critical — the difference between a strike and an involuntary movement during arrest is often visible on the footage.

Identification

Mistaken identity

In group public-order incidents, identifying the actual person responsible is frequently disputed. CCTV, clothing evidence and witness positioning routinely shift the picture, particularly where multiple people are charged on the same set of facts.

Consent

Consent

Available in certain non-DV contexts — for example contact sport, mutual conduct in private settings — though not where the conduct exceeded what was consented to. Rarely raised on s 340 matters but available in narrow contexts.

Factual

Factual dispute

Often the answer isn't a legal defence — it's challenging what actually happened. Witness statements, CCTV and body-worn footage routinely diverge from the QP9 narrative. The brief is the fight, not the charge.

PPRA challenge

Lawfulness of arrest

If the arrest itself was unlawful — no reasonable suspicion under the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act, failure to identify as police, no lawful direction — the "execution of duty" element fails and the charge should drop to common assault at most. Body-worn camera footage is decisive here.

s 27

Mental health / capacity

Where the defendant was experiencing a mental health crisis — psychotic episode, severe intoxication affecting capacity, untreated condition — this can ground a defence of unsoundness of mind under s 27, or serve as strong mitigation on sentence. Particularly relevant for assaults on hospital and ambulance staff.

Just been charged?

What to do tonight.

If you've been charged with serious assault in the last 24 hours, do these seven things — in this order — before you do anything else. They protect your defence and your sentence.

01

Stay silent

Beyond confirming your name, address and date of birth, exercise your right to silence. Do not "explain" yourself to police. Anything you say will be used.

02

Call a lawyer first

Contact a specialist criminal lawyer before your first court date — not after. Early advice changes the trajectory of the matter.

03

Don't contact the complainant

Especially in DV-related matters. Any contact — including through third parties — can found a fresh charge for breach of bail or DVO contravention.

04

Gather references early

Begin collecting character references from employer, community, family. The earlier you start, the stronger they are at sentence.

05

Seek treatment

If substance abuse or mental health was a factor, start treatment now. Evidence of rehabilitation in progress is one of the most powerful tools at sentencing.

06

Get medical records

Where relevant — for example, a clean record of communicable diseases for spitting allegations, or hospital records from the night in question.

07

Write a file note

While your memory is fresh, write a detailed account of what happened. Give it to your lawyer — never to police. Date it. Don't show anyone else.

Beyond the courtroom

What a conviction actually costs you.

The penalty handed down on the day is only part of what a serious assault conviction means. The real cost is what follows you out of the courthouse — into job applications, travel plans, registrations and your Blue Card.

Permanent criminal record

A conviction for serious assault is a permanent criminal record. It cannot be expunged. It appears on standard police checks and on Working with Children (Blue Card) applications. Spent convictions legislation does not erase it — it just changes who can see it.

Employment

Many industries require police checks — healthcare, education, security, government, mining, finance, transport. A serious assault conviction can end careers in any of these. Many employment contracts require disclosure of charges as well as convictions.

Travel

Convictions for violence can prevent entry to the United States, Canada, Japan and other countries with strict character requirements. ESTA and ETA waivers may be refused. Visas already granted can be cancelled following a conviction.

Blue Card / WWC

Serious assault is a "disqualifying offence" for Blue Card purposes under the Working with Children (Risk Management and Screening) Act 2000. A conviction can mean automatic refusal — and any career involving children, vulnerable people or NDIS work is affected.

Firearms licence

A conviction for serious assault results in automatic cancellation of a firearms licence under the Weapons Act 1990 (Qld). Reapplication is restricted for years afterwards. Particularly significant for clients in agriculture, security and primary industry.

Professional registration

Nurses, doctors, teachers, lawyers, paramedics, real estate agents and other registered professionals must disclose charges and convictions to their registering body — AHPRA, the QCT, the QLS, REIQ. Conviction can mean suspension, conditions or deregistration.

Serious assault — FAQ

Straight answers. No legalese.

The questions clients ask first when they're charged under s 340. If yours isn't here, ring us — we'll answer it on the spot.

Will I go to jail for serious assault in Queensland? +

Imprisonment is a realistic risk on s 340 — particularly for assault police variants involving bodily fluid, weapons or being in company. Maximums are 14 years for those variants and 7 years for others.

However, jail is not the only outcome. Probation, intensive correction orders and fines are achievable for first-time and lower-end matters. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are available where the conduct sits at the bottom of the s 340 range. Preparation makes the difference.

What is the difference between serious assault and common assault? +

Common assault (s 335) is the base offence — max 3 years. Serious assault (s 340) is what a common assault becomes when one of seven circumstances applies: police complainant, public officer, person 60+, person with disability, in company, resisting arrest, or corrections context.

The conduct can be identical. The classification — and therefore the maximum penalty — is driven by the circumstance.

Can I get a no-conviction result for serious assault? +

Yes — in the right matter. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are achievable for first-time s 340 matters where the conduct is at the lower end of the scale, the complainant suffered no significant injury, and the client presents well at sentence.

We invest heavily in references, rehabilitation material and psychological reports where they support the application. It's not automatic — but it's not rare either.

What if I was defending myself against a police officer? +

Self-defence is available even where the complainant is a police officer — but only where the officer's use of force was unlawful or disproportionate. We assess the lawfulness of the arrest, body-worn camera footage and the prior conduct before advising.

This is one of the most factually-sensitive areas in s 340 work. Don't make assumptions — let us look at the footage first.

How much does a serious assault lawyer cost? +

Clarity Law quotes a fixed fee in writing before any work begins. Magistrates Court pleas start in the low-to-mid thousands. District Court matters are quoted individually after the brief is reviewed. See our fees page for the indicative ranges.

Will my matter go to the District Court? +

Many s 340 matters are dealt with summarily in the Magistrates Court at the prosecution's election. Matters with bodily fluid, weapons, in-company aggravation, or causing bodily harm are committed to the District Court for trial or sentence.

Whether the matter stays in Magistrates is part of the negotiation we run early. Read our case-conferencing piece →

What should I do if I've just been charged with serious assault? +

Exercise your right to silence beyond confirming your identity, contact a specialist criminal lawyer before your first court date, do not contact the complainant, begin gathering character references, and seek treatment if substance abuse or mental health was a factor.

Write a detailed file note of what happened while your memory is fresh — and give it to your lawyer, not police. We've set out the full seven-step list in the What to do tonight section above.

Can a serious assault charge be downgraded? +

Yes. Through case conferencing with the prosecution, a serious assault charge can sometimes be negotiated down to common assault (s 335), which carries a lower maximum penalty and far better sentencing prospects — including realistic no-conviction outcomes and avoiding Blue Card consequences.

The window for this work is narrow — usually between brief disclosure and election. We routinely run these conversations early in the matter.

Will a serious assault conviction affect my job? +

A conviction appears on police checks and can affect employment in healthcare, education, security, government, mining, and any role requiring a Blue Card. It may also trigger mandatory disclosure to professional registration bodies — AHPRA, the Queensland College of Teachers, the Queensland Law Society.

This is why no-conviction outcomes matter so much on s 340 matters — they protect the future, not just the present.

What is the difference between serious assault and AOBH? +

Serious assault (s 340) is about WHO was assaulted or the CIRCUMSTANCES — police, person over 60, in company, resisting arrest. AOBH (s 339) is about the INJURY caused — does the assault interfere with health or comfort?

The same incident can potentially be charged as either — or both. Where injury is present and the complainant is in a protected category, prosecutors sometimes run both s 340 and s 339 in the alternative.

Can I represent myself on a serious assault charge? +

Technically yes — but serious assault is an indictable offence carrying up to 14 years. Self-represented defendants consistently receive harsher outcomes. The court process, evidence rules, prosecution negotiation and sentencing submissions all require specialist knowledge.

If cost is the concern, ring us first. Fixed-fee pricing exists for exactly this reason — knowing the number upfront makes the decision easier.

What is a section 12 no-conviction order? +

Under section 12 of the Penalties and Sentences Act 1992 (Qld), the court can find a person guilty but exercise its discretion not to record a conviction. This protects your criminal record, employment, travel and Blue Card status.

It is available for some serious assault matters, particularly first offences at the lower end of the scale. The application turns on the nature of the offence, your character, age and the impact a conviction would have on your future.
