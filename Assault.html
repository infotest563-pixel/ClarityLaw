01

Fight it — defences in play

Before any plea, we test whether the prosecution can actually prove the charge. On assault matters, real defences exist and are regularly missed.

  • Self-defence & defence of another (s 271)
  • Provocation (s 269)
  • Accident & lack of intent
  • Consent (for non-DV matters)
  • Identification issues & CCTV analysis
02

Downgrade it — charge negotiation

Where a total defence isn't realistic, the question becomes: is the charge overstated? GBH dropped to AOBH, AOBH dropped to common assault — these negotiations routinely change the sentencing range entirely.

  • Pre-charge representations
  • Briefs of evidence reviewed line-by-line
  • Medical reports & injury analysis
  • Prosecutor-level negotiation
03

Minimise it — sentencing strategy

When the matter is ultimately going on a plea, the work shifts to the sentence. No-conviction outcomes on assault happen — but they don't happen by accident.

  • Section 12 (no conviction) submissions
  • Character references & rehabilitation material
  • Psychological / medical reports where relevant
  • Apology letters & restorative options

Will I go to jail for an assault charge in Queensland? +

Not automatically. Whether jail is a realistic outcome depends on which assault you're charged with (common assault, AOBH, GBH, serious assault, etc.), your criminal history, the circumstances, and the severity of any injuries.

Many assault matters — especially first offences, common assault, and minor AOBH — resolve with a fine, probation, or a good-behaviour bond, often with no conviction recorded where we can argue section 12. For indictable matters like GBH, jail becomes a realistic consideration, which is why preparation from day one matters.

Can I get a "no conviction recorded" outcome for assault? +

Yes — it's possible in many cases. Under section 12 of the Penalties and Sentences Act, the court can impose a penalty without recording a conviction. It's most often available for common assault and minor AOBH matters with no (or limited) prior history.

The court considers the nature of the offence, the offender, and the impact of a conviction on employment, travel and personal circumstances. Well-prepared submissions, character references and rehabilitation material make a real difference.

What is the difference between common assault, AOBH and GBH? +

Common assault (s 335) — threat or application of force without actual injury. Max penalty 3 years. Usually dealt with in the Magistrates Court.

Assault occasioning bodily harm (s 339) — assault that causes actual injury that interferes with health or comfort (bruising, cuts, swelling). Max 7 years. Heard in Magistrates unless aggravated.

Grievous bodily harm (s 320) — serious or life-threatening injury, permanent injury, or loss of a distinct part or organ. Max 14 years. Always dealt with in the District Court.

At Clarity Law we quote a fixed fee in writing before we start — no hourly rates, no surprise bills. The fee depends on the charge, the court, and whether it's a plea or a trial.

Should I plead guilty to assault? +

Never decide before you get advice. We review the full brief of evidence — statements, CCTV, medical reports — before advising on plea.

Self-defence, provocation, accident, and factual disputes are all worth exploring first. Even if a plea is ultimately the right move, there's almost always room to negotiate the charge down, or to present the facts in a way that meaningfully reduces the sentence.

What should I do if I'm being questioned by police? +

You have the right to silence. You are only required to provide your name, date of birth and address. Beyond that, politely decline to answer questions until you have spoken to a lawyer.

Do not try to explain your side, apologise, or "set the record straight" — these statements almost always end up in the prosecution brief and are used against you. Call us immediately; we'll guide you through the next 24 hours.

Can I claim self-defence? +

Yes — self-defence is a complete defence to assault under sections 271 and 272 of the Queensland Criminal Code. If successful, you are acquitted.

It requires that you believed you (or another person) were under unlawful attack, that your response was a reasonable one in the circumstances, and that the force used was not disproportionate. The prosecution has to disprove self-defence beyond reasonable doubt. Whether it's viable depends entirely on the facts — we can tell you after one conversation.

Will an assault charge affect my employment, visa or Blue Card? +

It can. A recorded conviction for assault will appear on a National Police Check and can affect Blue Cards, Yellow Cards, nursing and teacher registration, visas, and many professional licences.

This is exactly why a section 12 no-conviction outcome is so valuable — and why we invest heavily in sentencing strategy. We routinely represent nurses, teachers, tradies with Blue Cards, aviation workers and visa holders where the stakes go well beyond the court penalty.

What happens at my first court appearance for assault? +

Your first appearance is called a mention. You don't need to enter a plea — and in most cases you shouldn't. We'll typically ask for an adjournment to obtain the brief of evidence, review it properly, and advise you on whether to fight, negotiate or plead.

If you've been charged and put on bail, we'll attend court with you on the day. If you've been remanded, we'll appear at your bail application first.
