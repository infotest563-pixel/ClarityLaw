What is serious assault?

Defined by who, not by how hard.

Section 340 of the Criminal Code 1899 (Qld) doesn't punish a different kind of violence. It punishes the same kind of violence — pushing, striking, threatening, spitting — when the person on the receiving end falls within a protected group, or when it happens in specified circumstances. The offence exists to shield the people society relies on to do difficult, dangerous or vulnerable work, and to protect older Queenslanders and people with disabilities from being targeted.

The most common version of the charge is assault on a police officer in the execution of their duty. Spitting at an officer during an arrest, kicking out while being put in handcuffs, pushing past an officer to leave the scene, biting during a search — every one of those sits inside the charge. So does pushing a paramedic responding to a triple-zero call, shoving a transit officer at a station, or grabbing the wrist of a corrections officer in a watchhouse. The conduct does not have to cause injury.

The other variants follow the same logic. An assault on a person aged 60 or over is automatically serious assault — there's no requirement that the offender knew the person's age, and there's no minimum force threshold. An assault on a person who relies on a guide dog, wheelchair or other remedial device is serious assault. So is an assault while in company with one or more other people, and an assault committed with intent to resist or prevent the lawful arrest of yourself or someone else.

Because the charge turns on circumstance rather than severity, people are routinely charged with serious assault for incidents they themselves describe as a scuffle. A push during an argument with an older neighbour. A reflex movement while being arrested for something else. A spit in frustration in the back of a divisional van. The paperwork that lands in your hands says "serious assault — up to 14 years," and it is genuinely terrifying. But the maximum is rarely the outcome.

Elements of the offence

What the prosecution must prove.

The prosecution must establish each element beyond reasonable doubt. If any one falls over, the charge fails — or drops to common assault.

1

An assault occurred.

Force was applied or threatened without consent — even minimal contact like a push or spit can qualify. No injury is required.

2

Protected category or circumstance.

The complainant was a police officer, public officer, aged 60+, has a disability — or the assault was in company or to resist arrest.

3

Officer acting in duty.

For police variants, the officer must have been performing their duties lawfully at the time.

4

No defence available.

The Crown must negate self-defence, accident and provocation where those defences are raised.

What makes an assault 'serious'

Seven circumstances that change the charge.

Some are about who the alleged victim is. Some are about when or how the assault occurred. Each one pushes the maximum penalty up — and changes which court hears the matter.

01

Police officer on duty

Assault of a police officer in the execution of their duty — including biting, spitting, kicking or pushing during arrest. Prosecution leads body-worn camera, the QP9 and the officer's statement.

Example: kicking out at an officer while being placed in handcuffs.
→
02

Public officer

Paramedics, ED nurses, corrections officers, transit officers, parking inspectors and other public officials acting in the course of their duty. The role is usually treated as intuitive.

Example: pushing a paramedic responding to a call-out.
→
03

Person aged 60 or over

Any assault where the complainant is 60 years or older becomes serious assault, regardless of context. The prosecution does not need to prove you knew their age.

Example: a shove during a parking dispute with an older neighbour.
→
04

Person with a disability

Assault of a person who relies on a guide dog, wheelchair or other remedial device. Argued whenever a mobility aid or assistance animal was visibly in use during the incident.

Example: minor contact during an argument with a person using a mobility aid.
→
05

While in company

Where the assault was committed in company with one or more other people — group dynamics elevate the conduct. Often raised in pub, party and street-fight matters with multiple defendants.

Example: two friends jointly involved in pushing a third person.
→
06

Resisting / preventing arrest

Assault committed with intent to resist or prevent the lawful arrest or detention of yourself or another person. Prosecution typically pairs this with obstruct police on the same set of facts.

Example: pulling away forcefully during arrest for a separate offence.
→
07

Corrections / unlawful conspiracy

Prisoners who assault working corrective services officers are separately covered under s 340(2). Section 340(1)(f) also captures assault committed in pursuance of an unlawful conspiracy.

Example: an assault on a corrections officer during a watchhouse search.

In practice

First-time matters dealt with in the Magistrates Court commonly result in probation, community service or fines. Wholly suspended sentences are routinely imposed for mid-range matters with strong rehabilitation. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are achievable at the lower end of the scale — and we invest in the application early.

Which court will hear my matter?

Magistrates Court or District Court?

This is the question every person charged asks within 24 hours. The honest answer is: the prosecution decides, not you — but how the matter is presented early can change which way they go.

Summary jurisdiction

Magistrates Court

Many matters CAN be dealt with summarily in the Magistrates Court at the prosecution's election under s 552A. This is almost always the better outcome — faster, cheaper, lower sentencing range.

  • Heard by a Magistrate alone — no jury
  • Lower-range maximum, often capped at 3 years for summary
  • Resolution typically in weeks to a few months
  • Most assault police matters without bodily fluid stay here
Typical timeline6–16 weeks
Higher jurisdiction

District Court

Where the conduct is more serious, the prosecution elects to commit the matter to the District Court for trial or sentence. Committal proceedings precede this — a Magistrates process where the brief is tested.

  • Heard by a Judge — jury for trials
  • Full 14-year maximum in play for indictable variants
  • Adds 8–12 months to the timeline after committal
  • Required for bodily fluid, weapons, in-company, BH causing
Typical timeline9–18 months

Why this matters: whether your matter stays in the Magistrates Court is something Clarity Law negotiates with the prosecution early — before the brief lands, before the election is made. Defences we run

Real defences. Tested every week.

We review the brief of evidence, body-worn camera footage, CCTV and witness statements before advising on your options. The right defence often turns on what the police footage actually shows — not what the QP9 says. Hover any card.

s 271 / 272

Self-defence

Available even against police where the use of force by the officer was unlawful or disproportionate. The prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt. Body-worn footage and witness statements often reframe what looked like assault into reasonable response.

↗
s 269

Provocation

Partial defence reducing intent. Considered carefully where there has been sustained antagonism prior to the alleged assault, or where racial, sexual or homophobic abuse preceded the contact.

↗
s 23

Accident or lack of intent

Contact made while struggling against an unlawful restraint, where intent to assault is denied. Body-worn camera review is critical — the difference between a strike and an involuntary movement during arrest is often visible on the footage.

↗
Identification

Mistaken identity

In group public-order incidents, identifying the actual person responsible is frequently disputed. CCTV, clothing evidence and witness positioning routinely shift the picture, particularly where multiple people are charged on the same set of facts.

↗
Consent

Consent

Available in certain non-DV contexts — for example contact sport, mutual conduct in private settings — though not where the conduct exceeded what was consented to. Rarely raised but available in narrow contexts.

↗
Factual

Factual dispute

Often the answer isn't a legal defence — it's challenging what actually happened. Witness statements, CCTV and body-worn footage routinely diverge from the QP9 narrative. The brief is the fight, not the charge.

↗
PPRA

Lawfulness of arrest

If the arrest was unlawful — no reasonable suspicion under the Police Powers and Responsibilities Act, failure to identify as police, no lawful direction — the "execution of duty" element fails and the charge should drop to common assault at most.

↗
s 27

Mental health · capacity

Where the defendant was experiencing a mental health crisis — psychotic episode, severe intoxication affecting capacity, untreated condition — this can ground a defence of unsoundness of mind, or serve as strong mitigation on sentence.

Just been charged?

Don't do these wrong.

If you've been charged in the last 24 hours, do these eight things — in this order — before you do anything else. They protect your defence and your sentence.

Tonight's list.
Tear off · keep visible · don't share
  1. Stay silent. Beyond confirming your name, address and date of birth, exercise your right to silence. Do not "explain" yourself to police — anything you say will be used.
  2. Call a lawyer first. Contact a criminal lawyer before your first court date — not after. Early advice changes the trajectory of the matter.
  3. Don't contact the complainant. Especially in DV-related matters. Any contact — including through third parties — can found a fresh charge for breach of bail or DVO contravention.
  4. Gather references early. Begin collecting character references from employer, community, family. The earlier you start, the stronger they are at sentence. A lawyer can give you a guide to character references in court.
  5. Seek treatment. If substance abuse or mental health was a factor, start treatment now. Evidence of rehabilitation in progress is one of the most powerful tools at sentencing.
  6. Get medical records. Where relevant — for example, a clean record of communicable diseases for spitting allegations, or hospital records from the night in question.
  7. Write a file note. While your memory is fresh, write a detailed account of what happened. Give it to your lawyer — never to police. Date it. Don't show anyone else.

Beyond the courtroom

What a conviction actually costs you.

The penalty handed down on the day is only part of what a conviction means. The real cost is what follows you out of the courthouse.

  • Permanent record

    A conviction stays on your criminal history. Spent convictions legislation does not erase it — it just changes who can see it.

  • Employment

    Police checks gate-keep healthcare, education, security, government, finance and transport roles.

  • Travel

    Convictions for violence can prevent entry to the US, Canada and Japan. Existing visas can be cancelled.

  • Blue Card & NDIS

    Serious assault is a disqualifying offence under the Working with Children Act — a conviction can mean automatic refusal.

  • Firearms licence

    Automatic cancellation under the Weapons Act 1990 (Qld), with reapplication restricted for years.

  • Professional registration

    AHPRA, the QCT and the QLS must be told. Conviction can mean conditions, suspension or deregistration.

Will I go to jail for serious assault in Queensland? +

Imprisonment is a realistic risk — particularly for assault police variants involving bodily fluid, weapons or being in company. Maximums are 14 years for those variants and 7 years for others.

However, jail is not the only outcome. Probation, intensive correction orders and fines are achievable for first-time and lower-end matters. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are available where the conduct sits at the bottom of the scale.

What is the difference between serious assault and common assault? +

Common assault (s 335) is the base offence — max 3 years. Serious assault is what a common assault becomes when one of seven circumstances applies: police complainant, public officer, person 60+, person with disability, in company, resisting arrest, or corrections context.

The conduct can be identical. The classification — and therefore the maximum penalty — is driven by the circumstance.

Can I get a no-conviction result for serious assault? +

Yes — in the right matter. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are achievable for first-time matters where the conduct is at the lower end of the scale, the complainant suffered no significant injury, and the client presents well at sentence.

We invest heavily in references, rehabilitation material and psychological reports. It's not automatic — but it's not rare either.

What if I was defending myself against a police officer? +

Self-defence is available even where the complainant is a police officer — but only where the officer's use of force was unlawful or disproportionate. We assess the lawfulness of the arrest, body-worn camera footage and the prior conduct before advising.

This is one of the most factually-sensitive areas in our work. Don't make assumptions — let us look at the footage first.

How much does a serious assault lawyer cost? +

Clarity Law quotes a fixed fee in writing before any work begins. Magistrates Court pleas start in the low-to-mid thousands. District Court matters are quoted individually after the brief is reviewed. See our fees page.

Will my matter go to the District Court? +

Many matters are dealt with summarily in the Magistrates Court at the prosecution's election. Matters with bodily fluid, weapons, in-company aggravation, or causing bodily harm are committed to the District Court for trial or sentence.

What should I do if I've just been charged? +

Exercise your right to silence beyond confirming your identity, contact a specialist criminal lawyer before your first court date, do not contact the complainant, begin gathering character references, and seek treatment if substance abuse or mental health was a factor.

The full eight-step list is in the Tonight's list section above.

Can a serious assault charge be downgraded? +

Yes. Through case conferencing with the prosecution, the charge can sometimes be negotiated down to common assault (s 335), which carries a lower maximum and far better sentencing prospects — including realistic no-conviction outcomes and avoiding Blue Card consequences.

Will a conviction affect my job? +

A conviction appears on police checks and can affect employment in healthcare, education, security, government, mining, and any role requiring a Blue Card. It may also trigger mandatory disclosure to professional registration bodies — AHPRA, the Queensland College of Teachers, the Queensland Law Society.

What's the difference between serious assault and AOBH? +

Serious assault is about WHO was assaulted or the CIRCUMSTANCES — police, person over 60, in company, resisting arrest. AOBH (s 339) is about the INJURY caused.

The same incident can potentially be charged as either — or both.

Can I represent myself? +

Technically yes — but the charge is indictable and carries up to 14 years. Self-represented defendants consistently receive harsher outcomes. The court process, evidence rules, prosecution negotiation and sentencing submissions all require specialist knowledge.

What is a section 12 no-conviction order? +

Under section 12 of the Penalties and Sentences Act 1992 (Qld), the court can find a person guilty but exercise its discretion not to record a conviction. This protects your criminal record, employment, travel and Blue Card status.
