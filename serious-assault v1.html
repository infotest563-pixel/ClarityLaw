What makes an assault 'serious'

Six circumstances that change the charge.

Section 340 of the Criminal Code lists the circumstances that elevate a common assault to serious assault. Some are about who the alleged victim is. Some are about when the assault occurred. Both push the maximum penalty up.

01

Police officer on duty

Assault of a police officer in execution of their duty — including biting, spitting, kicking or pushing during arrest. The most prosecuted variant of s 340.

Example: kicking out at an officer while being placed in handcuffs after a public-order incident.
02

Public officer

Paramedics, nurses in emergency departments, correctional officers, transit officers, parking inspectors and other public officials acting in the course of their duty.

Example: pushing a paramedic responding to a call-out, even where no injury results.
03

Person aged 60 or over

Any assault where the complainant is 60 years or older becomes serious assault, regardless of context.

Example: a shove during an argument with an older neighbour over a parking dispute.
04

Person with a disability

Assault of a person who relies on a guide dog, wheelchair or other remedial device — recognised under s 340.

Example: minor contact during an argument with a person who uses a mobility aid.
05

While in company

Where the assault was committed in company with one or more other people — group dynamics elevate the conduct.

Example: two friends jointly involved in pushing a third person during a confrontation.
06

Resisting / preventing arrest

Assault committed with intent to resist or prevent the lawful arrest or detention of yourself or another person.

Example: pulling away forcefully during an arrest for a separate offence.

Defences we run on serious assault

Real defences. Tested every week.

We review the brief of evidence, body-worn camera footage, CCTV and witness statements before advising on your options. The right defence on a serious assault matter often turns on what the police footage actually shows — not what the QP9 says.

s 271 / 272

Self-defence

Available even against police where the use of force by the officer was unlawful or disproportionate. The prosecution must disprove it beyond reasonable doubt.

s 269

Provocation

Partial defence reducing intent. Considered carefully where there has been sustained antagonism prior to the alleged assault.

s 23

Accident / lack of intent

For example, contact made while struggling against an unlawful restraint, where intent to assault is denied. Body-worn camera review is critical.

Identification

Mistaken identity

In group public-order incidents, identifying the actual person responsible is frequently disputed. CCTV and clothing evidence routinely shift the picture.

Consent

Consent

Available in certain non-DV contexts — for example contact sport, mutual conduct in private settings — though not where the conduct exceeded what was consented to.

Factual

Factual dispute

Often the answer isn't a legal defence — it's challenging what actually happened. Witness statements and CCTV often diverge from the prosecution narrative.

Serious assault — FAQ

Straight answers. No legalese.

The questions clients ask first when they're charged under s 340. If yours isn't here, ring us — we'll answer it on the spot.

Will I go to jail for serious assault in Queensland? +

Imprisonment is a realistic risk on s 340 — particularly for assault police variants involving bodily fluid, weapons or being in company. Maximums are 14 years for those variants and 7 years for others.

However, jail is not the only outcome. Probation, intensive correction orders and fines are achievable for first-time and lower-end matters. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are available where the conduct sits at the bottom of the s 340 range. Preparation makes the difference.

What is the difference between serious assault and common assault? +

Common assault (s 335) is the base offence — max 3 years. Serious assault (s 340) is what a common assault becomes when one of six circumstances applies: police complainant, public officer, person 60+, person with disability, in company, or resisting arrest.

The conduct can be identical. The classification — and therefore the maximum penalty — is driven by the circumstance.

Can I get a no-conviction result for serious assault? +

Yes — in the right matter. Section 12 no-conviction outcomes are achievable for first-time s 340 matters where the conduct is at the lower end of the scale, the complainant suffered no significant injury, and the client presents well at sentence.

We invest heavily in references, rehabilitation material and psychological reports where they support the application. It's not automatic — but it's not rare either.

What if I was defending myself against a police officer? +

Self-defence is available even where the complainant is a police officer — but only where the officer's use of force was unlawful or disproportionate. We assess the lawfulness of the arrest, body-worn camera footage and the prior conduct before advising.

This is one of the most factually-sensitive areas in s 340 work. Don't make assumptions — let us look at the footage first.

How much does a serious assault lawyer cost? +

Clarity Law quotes a fixed fee in writing before any work begins. Magistrates Court pleas start in the low-to-mid thousands. District Court matters are quoted individually after the brief is reviewed. See our fees page for the indicative ranges.

Will my matter go to the District Court? +

Many s 340 matters are dealt with summarily in the Magistrates Court at the prosecution's election. Matters with bodily fluid, weapons, in-company aggravation, or causing bodily harm are committed to the District Court for trial or sentence.

Whether the matter stays in Magistrates is part of the negotiation we run early.
